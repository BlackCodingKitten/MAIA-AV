\section{Preliminary Analysis Results}
\label{sec:preliminary_analysis_results}
\subsection*{Expected Behaviour under the Preliminary Condition}
\label{subsec:preliminary_expectations}
Before examining the experimental outcomes, it is useful to lay out qualitative expectations for model performance under the frame-only condition. This provides a methodological anchor for interpreting distinctions across question categories, rather than deriving explanations retrospectively from the results themselves.
For spatial questions, a relatively high presence of visual evidence is expected, given the inherent visibility of entity locations, movements, object configurations, and spatial relationships within the visual stream. Complexity may increase, however, when this information must be reconstructed across multiple temporal intervals, as when tracking an entity through a sequence of movements or deriving a spatial configuration that results from several successive actions.
Temporal questions are expected to display a more varied profile. Visually distinctive actions and event sequences can often be pinpointed through timestamp data and organized by the temporal structure produced during Event Analysis, but not all temporal information is necessarily recoverable from visual input alone. Some questions may involve events whose interpretation depends on linguistic context, or on relations between moments that remain ambiguous from visual information alone. The adequacy of the resulting representation therefore hinges on the quality of chronological reconstruction as much as on the inherent nature of the events involved.
Causal questions are anticipated to be the most demanding under the visual-only condition. The visual stream can capture event succession, interactions, and observable state changes, but the underlying reasons for these events often depend on spoken dialogue or remain simply indistinguishable from static imagery. Motivations, intentions, and explanatory relationships are less likely to be directly supported by visual evidence alone. This expectation also underlines a methodological distinction maintained throughout the analysis: temporal succession is not treated as evidence of causation.
These expectations are formed independently of the empirical results and serve only as a qualitative reference. No claim is made about the magnitude of category-level differences or about which category will ultimately score highest.
Proficiency results are then compared with downstream Visual Statement Verification (VSV) performance, in order to separate evidence availability from task execution. Visual proficiency asks whether the independently constructed representation contains enough information to distinguish a caption from its foil. VSV performance asks whether the model produces the correct response once the question and the condition-specific inputs are directly available. Together, the two measures offer a diagnostic view that plain task accuracy cannot provide on its own.
A Pass proficiency result paired with a correct VSV response indicates that the relevant visual evidence was both represented and successfully used downstream. A Pass result paired with an incorrect response instead points to a failure occurring after the evidence was already available, in selection, integration, or later reasoning. When both proficiency and VSV performance are negative, the most plausible explanation is a perceptual or representational shortfall, though this comparison alone cannot pinpoint the exact locus of failure.
The most diagnostically informative configuration is the opposite one: proficiency Not Pass paired with a correct downstream answer. In this context, the model opts for the correct alternative even if the visual depiction fails to meet the proficiency standard. One plausible rationale is that the prediction harnesses partial visual indications, parametric priors, heuristic strategies, or random guessing at chance level. These elements are scrutinized more thoroughly in Section~\ref{subsec:no_input}. However, this phenomenon might also suggest effective multimodal integration, where inadequacies in retrieving information from the visual stream are offset by evidence accessible through another input modality. Such interpretations should be approached prudently. The distinction between PASS and NOT PASS arises from a score given by an automatic evaluator based on a defined heuristic criterion and should not be viewed as a flawless evaluation of information accessibility. Instead, it serves as a diagnostic baseline for contrasting the initial representation with the model's ensuing behavioral performance.
\subsection*{Entity, Event, and Inference Recovery}
\label{subsec:entity_event_inference}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pa}, the preliminary analysis asks whether the visual content of a video alone allows a model to recover the information required to answer a question correctly. It also serves an independent diagnostic role by verifying that the newly constructed questions do not inherit the same limitation as the original MAIA benchmark \cite{testa2025maia}, namely a dependence on visual information strong enough to reduce the possibility of assessing genuine cross-modal integration.

The analysis targets an earlier stage of the reasoning process than VSV itself, namely the semantic construction of what has been observed. This makes it possible to determine whether a model can independently extract from the video the entities, events, and inferential relations required by the question. The resulting representation separates information grounded in visual evidence from answers that may instead be supported by linguistic priors.

Entity-level information remains the most accessible representational component for all four models. At this level, the model primarily needs to associate a perceptually available element with its semantic representation, establishing referential grounding between an observed entity and the lexical concept used to describe it. The two larger models obtain the strongest results, with entity-recovery scores of 34.47 for Qwen-30B and 33.77 for Gemma-12B. The smaller Gemma-4B    model follows the same pattern at a lower absolute level, reaching 27.87.
Qwen-3B  represents a clear outlier. Its entity score reaches only 2.77, followed by 2.07 for events and 0.95 for inference, resulting in an overall comprehension score of 2.18. The model therefore remains close to the lower bound of the evaluation despite preserving the same entity--event--inference ordering observed in the other architectures. This result indicates that only a very limited portion of the question-relevant visual information is recovered by its preliminary representation.
The decline from entity recovery to inference recovery is visible in every model and reflects increasing representational demands. Event recovery requires individually detected entities to be integrated into dynamic structures involving actions, participants, and state transitions. This introduces a predicate--argument structure that extends beyond simple referential grounding. Inference recovery requires an additional transformation, since the model must derive relations or interpretations that do not necessarily correspond to a directly observable perceptual pattern. These may involve temporal ordering, causal interpretation, state dependencies, or relations connecting multiple observed events.
Qwen-30B moves from 34.47 for entities to 31.53 for events and 23.95 for inferences. Gemma-12B follows a similar pattern, decreasing from 33.77 to 30.07 and then to 21.48. Gemma-4B    decreases from 27.87 to 23.13 and finally to 16.48. The same hierarchy is present in Qwen-3B , although at substantially lower values, moving from 2.77 to 2.07 and finally to 0.95.
The analysis highlights two complementary effects pertaining to representational complexity. Firstly, an increase in this complexity systematically diminishes the volume of retrievable information. Accurate entity identification does not necessarily imply that the associated event is represented, nor does the successful recovery of an event ensure the accurate reconstruction of the relations required by the query. Secondly, larger model variants within both families retrieve significantly more information compared to their smaller versions. This disparity is particularly evident in the Qwen model family. However, it is important to interpret the notably low Qwen-3B  values with caution, as model size alone does not entirely determine the quality and completeness of the intermediate structured representation. Qwen2.5-Omni-3B demonstrated a heightened sensitivity to prompt formulation and input text handling during experimental analyses. Given that the experimental protocol mandated the consistent use of the same prompt across all four models, adaptations were made as much as possible to accommodate the formatting constraints intrinsic to Qwen2.5-Omni-3B without modifying the shared prompt itself. Despite these measures, a considerable number of generation errors related to prompts were still encountered. Therefore, the anomalously low scores observed in this model may, in part, be attributed to instabilities in prompt processing and generation, rather than solely indicating a true deficiency in its capacity to recover visual information.

Spatial configurations can often be recovered directly from visual evidence, especially by Qwen-30B. Causal questions often provide sufficiently salient entities and events, but the inferential link between them remains substantially less recoverable. Qwen-3B  reproduces the same general hierarchy at markedly lower absolute values, indicating that the structure of the task remains visible even when the amount of successfully recovered information is minimal.
\subsection*{Preliminar Spatial Information}
\label{subsec:preliminary_spatial}
Spatial questions are the most successful category for Qwen-30B, reaching 41.50 on entity level, 40.50 on event level, and 37.05 on inference level, for a total comprehension score of 38.85. The relatively small drop from one level to the next is notable in itself: once the required entities have been retrieved, the model retains enough relational information to reconstruct the spatial configuration the question asks for, suggesting that spatial relations are often grounded directly in observable image content.
The same trend appears for Gemma-12B, at lower absolute values: 35.90 for entities, 33.20 for events, and 28.25 for inferences. Gemma-4B    reaches 29.10, 26.10, and 22.60 respectively. This category appears to benefit from the perceptual nature of its target relations: locations, relative positions, trajectories, and object configurations can often be detected within a limited temporal window, keeping the semantic relation the question targets close to perceptual structure.
Spatial questions require relating lexicalized spatial concepts, location, direction, containment, proximity, movement, to geometrical or relational structures in the scene, which in turn requires correctly retrieving the referents and the relation between them. The stronger performance of Qwen-30B in this category indicates that its visual representations retain more of this relational structure. Spatial grounding is nonetheless not always successful: Qwen-30B's overall comprehension score stays under 40, and correctly detecting an object does not guarantee that the required relation is also retrieved, particularly when it depends on tracking the entity over time.
\subsection*{Preliminar Temporal Information}
\label{subsec:preliminary_temporal}
Of all categories, temporal questions show the most degraded performance for every model tested: 10.60 for Gemma, 15.05 for Gemma-12B, and 13.45 for Qwen-30B. This decline is not confined to inference. Entity and event representation are already low for Gemma, at 15.50 and 12.50, while Gemma-12B reaches 20.10 and 19.40, and Qwen-30B reaches 18.30 and 17.60. Inference scores are lower still: 7.35, 10.50, and 9.30 respectively.
Temporal difficulty, in other words, is not confined to the final reasoning step. The initial representations already fail to recover one or more of the entities or events needed to reconstruct the temporal relation itself. A temporal question typically depends on at least two event anchors that must be identified individually and then arranged relative to each other; missing one of them prevents the relation from being restored even when the other has been correctly identified. Qualitative inspection confirms this pattern: the model often recognizes the entities involved but fails to anchor one of the two events precisely enough to support relations such as \textit{before}, \textit{after}, \textit{first}, or \textit{second}.
This points to a demand beyond simple lexical grounding: events need to be represented in their structural semantic form and placed within a shared temporal context, which is exactly what the low inference scores reflect. This is consistent with the original MAIA study, where temporal phenomena were especially difficult to resolve under visual-only evaluation \cite{testa2025maia}, and with benchmarks such as VILMA, which treat temporal grounding as a capability distinct from static visual recognition.
\subsection*{Preliminar Causal Information}
\label{subsec:preliminary_causal}
The causal category produces a more varied picture than expected at first glance. Causal questions do not receive the lowest scores; Gemma-4B    and Gemma-12B in fact achieve their highest scores in this category, at 25.79 and 31.75 respectively, and Qwen-30B reaches 30.95, only slightly below its spatial score.
This is not evidence that causality is visually easy, however. The score distribution tells a different story, with both models scoring far higher on entities and events than on inference. Gemma-12B reaches an entity score of 45.30 and an event score of 37.60, while its inference score drops to 25.70. Qwen-30B shows the same pattern, with scores of 43.60, 36.50, and 25.50 across the three levels. Gemma-4B    shows the sharpest proportional drop, from an entity score of 39.00 to 30.80 for events and 19.50 for inference. The visual stream, in short, usually contains enough information to identify the participants and actions involved in a causal situation, but representing \textit{why} something happens is considerably harder than representing \textit{what} happens.
This gap matters for how causal reasoning should be understood, since it depends on a relation between propositions or events rather than a property of a single event on its own. A model can correctly lexicalize an action and identify its participants without representing the explanatory link between that action and an antecedent state, intention, or motivation, especially when that link was conveyed only through speech and never shown visually. The visual representation may therefore contain the observable outcome while lacking the information needed for causal interpretation. This experiment accordingly distinguishes between \textit{causal event availability} and \textit{causal relation recovery}.

\section{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:experimental_results_discussion}
The experimental conditions offer a controlled view of how visual, auditory, and transcription-derived information contribute to Visual Statement Verification. Rather than reading multimodal performance purely in terms of absolute accuracy, the analysis below treats each condition as evidence of the informational resources available to a model and of its ability to exploit them consistently. Throughout, it draws on the preliminary analysis in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_analysis_results} to separate what a model can perceive from what it can do once perception is paired with a question.
The comparison is particularly relevant against the original MAIA results \cite{testa2025maia} and against the experimental setting introduced in \textit{Lost in Transcription} \cite{deodati2026lost}. MAIA was designed as a visually grounded benchmark, in which the distinction between a true statement and its minimally contrastive foil is expected to depend primarily on information extracted from the video. The original evaluation showed that increasing visual evidence generally improves performance, though gains vary across reasoning categories and temporal phenomena remain particularly difficult. \textit{Lost in Transcription} extended this setting to audio and transcription, finding that raw audio contributed substantially when visual evidence was absent but became almost neutral once dense visual information was available, and that adding textualized audio in the transcription-based setting even produced a small but systematic degradation.
The results reported here present a very different scenario across model families and scales.

\subsection*{No-Input Condition and Response Bias}
\label{subsec:no_input}
The no-input condition evaluates model behaviour when the question and the two
candidate statements are provided without video, raw audio, or transcription.
This setting calls for careful interpretation. Achieving a performance level significantly above chance indicates that the decision can be made, at least partially, without relying on perceptual evidence. However, it does not reveal the underlying mechanism responsible for this performance. \textbf{Above-chance
accuracy is not, by itself, evidence of linguistic plausibility}.
Prior work on multiple-choice evaluation supports this caution. Large
language models exhibit systematic selection biases toward particular answer
identifiers or positions, and their performance can shift substantially when
the same candidate answers are reordered
\cite{zheng2024largelanguagemodelsrobust,
pezeshkpour-hruschka-2024-large}.
For this reason, no-input accuracy needs to be read alongside the model's
response distribution, not on its own.

600 items have the correct answer in the first position and 600 in the second. Table~\ref{tab:no_input_bias} reports no-input accuracy

standard deviation of the two recalls ($RStd$). Following

selection bias, with larger values indicating a stronger asymmetry between

The four models exhibit markedly different behaviours in the no-input
condition. Qwen-3B provides the clearest example of a positional shortcut. Its
item accuracy remains close to chance at 50.83\%, while 99.00\% of its valid
predictions select the first candidate. Recall reaches 99.83\% when the first
candidate is correct and falls to 1.83\% when the second candidate is correct,
resulting in an $RStd$ of 49.00. Its near-chance accuracy therefore does not
reflect an unbiased discrimination between the two statements. Rather, it is
the consequence of an almost deterministic preference for the first answer
position on a position-balanced dataset.
Gemma-12B also displays a substantial positional asymmetry. The model selects
the first candidate in 77.35\% of valid predictions, with recalls of 86.33\%
and 32.17\% for the first and second answer positions respectively
($RStd = 27.08$). Gemma-4B    follows the same tendency, although less strongly,
with an $RStd$ of 14.08. In both cases, positional preference contributes to
the observed no-input performance and prevents aggregate accuracy from being
interpreted directly as evidence that the model is discriminating between the
semantic content of the two candidates.
Qwen-30B presents a different pattern. Its predictions are substantially more
balanced across answer positions, with an aggregate $RStd$ of only 6.50.
A fixed positional shortcut is therefore unlikely to explain its above-chance
performance. However, repeated evaluations carried out with exactly the same
experimental configuration and candidate ordering produced noticeably
different accuracies, approximately ranging from 55\% to 62\%. The observed
performance should consequently not be regarded as a stable deterministic
property of the model.
A plausible interpretation is that Qwen-30B exhibits a
\textbf{model-specific sensitivity to weak structural cues contained in the
contrastive candidate pairs}. These cues may correspond to subtle regularities
introduced by the construction of true statements and their corresponding
foils. This interpretation is distinct from a general linguistic plausibility
effect. If the correct candidate could be systematically identified through
the same overt linguistic regularities, a comparable advantage would be
expected across architectures. Instead, the effect appears strongly
model-dependent. The variation observed across repeated runs further suggests
that these weak cues do not determine the response consistently, but may bias
the decision when perceptual evidence is unavailable.

These results separate two phenomena that aggregate accuracy alone would
conflate. \textbf{Answer-position bias explains a substantial part of the
no-input behaviour of some models, but it cannot account for all above-chance
performance.} Conversely, above-chance accuracy in the absence of a strong
position bias does not demonstrate that the model has recovered information
relevant to the audiovisual event. In the case of Qwen-30B, the results are
instead compatible with a model-specific sensitivity to weaker structural
regularities in the contrastive pairs, although dedicated controls would be
required to identify their precise nature.
The no-input condition is therefore best interpreted as a diagnostic of
\emph{decision behaviour in the absence of perceptual grounding}. Depending on
the model, decisions may be dominated by a positional shortcut, influenced by
weak structural properties of the candidate pair, or exhibit substantial
instability across repeated inferences. No-input accuracy should consequently
not be treated as evidence of a single common linguistic bias, but as the
observable outcome of different model-specific strategies operating when the
perceptual evidence required by the task is removed.

This result matters because MAIA was originally developed as a visually grounded benchmark: its audio track played no role in constructing the questions and statements. Strong audio-only performance does not make the benchmark intrinsically audio-based: it shows instead that the acoustic stream carries information strongly correlated with the events and situations the questions query. Raw audio can supply several complementary sources of evidence at once. Spoken language can explicitly identify objects, actions, locations, intentions, or causal relations. Environmental sounds can signal ongoing events. Acoustic dynamics can offer temporal cues about occurrence or transition.
The linguistic component of speech is likely central to this advantage. Once speech is encoded, its semantic representation interfaces naturally with the language backbone that compares the two candidate statements, narrowing part of the representational gap between visual patterns and linguistic propositions. The result is best read as evidence of high \textit{informational accessibility} in the acoustic channel, not as proof that audio is inherently richer than vision: a visual scene may contain more information overall while still being harder for the architecture to convert into a representation aligned with the VSV decision.
The pattern differs markedly from \textit{Lost in Transcription}, where Qwen2.5-Omni-7B obtained 82.70\% with 32 visual frames and 82.68\% once raw audio was added, a negligible $-0.02$ point difference. There, raw audio became beneficial mainly once visual evidence was removed entirely, raising black-frame accuracy from 67.40\% to 73.19\% \cite{deodati2026lost}. Audio plays a substantially stronger role across the architectures evaluated here. Model-family effects, differences in the reasoning categories considered, and stronger audio exploitation in more recent omnimodal architectures could all contribute. The present results support the behavioural difference without isolating its architectural cause.

Raw audio outperforms transcription for every model, from 7.00 points for Qwen-3B down to 2.17 for Qwen-30B. The contribution of audio therefore cannot be reduced to a simple speech-to-text transformation. \textbf{Transcription preserves lexical and propositional content, but it strips out or compresses non-verbal sound, prosody, overlapping speakers, temporal acoustic events, and background activity.} The transcription pipeline can itself introduce recognition errors or drop uncertain material, so the textual representation is a lossy transformation of the original signal.
The gap between the small and large Qwen-3B configurations adds a further, linguistically informative pattern. Qwen-3B reaches 66.50\% with transcription alone, Qwen-30B reaches 77.00\%, a gap considerably larger than the 0.75-point difference the same two models show in video-only input. Increased model capacity is especially beneficial once information is already represented in linguistic form: a larger backbone exploits lexical semantics, discourse relations, event descriptions, and contextual associations more effectively than a smaller one, so scaling gains look more pronounced in language-mediated reasoning than in isolated visual grounding. This fits a broader distinction between perceptual encoding and linguistic inference. Once evidence has been converted to text, what remains is largely semantic selection and integration. Visual evidence, by contrast, must first be turned into a linguistic-semantic representation before the same kind of reasoning can even begin.
The pattern also contrasts with \textit{Lost in Transcription}, where Qwen2.5-VL-7B reached 68.72\% in the linguistic-only baseline but only 63.98\% with transcription alone: transcription failed to outperform the model's own linguistic prior \cite{deodati2026lost}. The models evaluated here instead gain substantially from transcription relative to their no-input baselines, particularly Qwen-30B. The ability to convert auxiliary textual evidence into useful task information appears to vary with scale rather than being a fixed property of an architecture.
\subsection*{Video-Only Performance and the Visual Bottleneck}
\label{subsec:video_only}
Video-only accuracy ranges from 69.83\% to 73.25\%, as already shown in Table~\ref{tab:overall_condition_performance}. All four models extract substantial task-relevant evidence from the sampled frames, yet the narrow range across architectures suggests a shared bottleneck in visual processing rather than a strongly scale-dependent one. Increasing model size from Qwen-3B to Qwen-30B produces only a 0.75-point gain in video-only accuracy, against a 10.50-point gap between the same two models in transcription-only accuracy. Linguistic scale therefore appears to improve more strongly the operations performed once relevant semantic information is already accessible, including lexical comparison, semantic compatibility assessment, integration of explicit contextual evidence, and reasoning over propositions, without producing an equivalent improvement in temporally distributed visual extraction.
The distinction becomes considerably sharper once video-only accuracy is compared with the preliminary analysis of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_analysis_results}. Overall visual comprehension there reaches only 20.46 for Gemma, 25.90 for Gemma-12B, and 27.75 for Qwen-30B, well below the 69.83\%, 73.08\%, and 73.25\% obtained by the same models in video-only VSV. This discrepancy reflects different informational regimes rather than a contradiction between the two evaluations.
The preliminary analysis constructs a representation without access to the downstream question and measures whether the entities, events, and relations later required by the task are spontaneously recovered from the visual input. In the video-only VSV condition, by contrast, the question and the two minimally contrastive alternatives explicitly identify the semantic distinction to be resolved. Visual processing can therefore be conditioned on a specific linguistic target, directing evidence selection toward the relevant entities, actions, temporal intervals, or relations. The restricted contrastive decision space also means that a complete semantic reconstruction of the video is not always necessary for a correct prediction. The distinction is therefore one between \textit{question-independent representation} and \textit{question-conditioned retrieval}.
A direct statistical comparison supports this interpretation. At the video level, correlations between preliminary comprehension and downstream video-only performance are generally weak. Gemma-12B is the only model showing a significant association, with $\rho=0.280$ for item accuracy ($p=.0048$), while the other models remain close to zero or non-significant. At the question level, however, stronger preliminary representations clearly increase the probability of downstream success. Item accuracy rises from 68.8\% to 80.8\% for Gemma, from 71.1\% to 85.4\% for Gemma-12B, and from 70.3\% to 87.5\% for Qwen-30B when moving from \textit{Not Pass} to \textit{Pass} preliminary representations. Qwen-30B also shows the strongest pool-level effect, increasing from 35.9\% to 67.3\% ($\mathrm{OR}=3.68$, $p<.001$).
These results show that richer question-independent visual evidence facilitates downstream reasoning but is not a necessary condition for correctness. This is particularly evident for Qwen-3B , which achieves 72.5\% video-only item accuracy despite having no preliminary representations classified as \textit{Pass}. Its preliminary scores are affected by an extreme floor effect and by the prompt-related generation faults discussed above, so their relationship with downstream performance cannot be interpreted in the same way as for the other models. More generally, the high accuracy retained under \textit{Not Pass} conditions shows that evidence not sufficiently recovered during the preliminary stage may still become accessible once the question constrains the retrieval process.
The original MAIA study offers a useful external reference point. With 32 frames, Qwen2.5-VL-7B obtained a pool-based VSV accuracy of 0.44, while Qwen2.5-VL-72B reached 0.54 \cite{testa2025maia}. This indicates a scaling advantage, but not full consistency: spatial, causal, and implicit categories benefited from richer visual evidence, while temporal reasoning remained comparatively weak. Direct numerical comparison with the present models must remain cautious because the current evaluation covers a different category subset and different architectures. The qualitative pattern is nevertheless compatible: stronger models can exploit visual evidence more effectively, but increased language-model capacity alone does not remove the difficulty of grounding temporally and relationally complex information. The preliminary analysis further localizes this limitation by showing that part of the bottleneck arises before downstream reasoning, at the stage at which relevant information is extracted and organized from the visual stream.

Transcription discards part of that information in exchange for direct access to lexical and propositional structure. Which of the two wins out for a given model depends on the balance it strikes between perceptual richness and linguistic accessibility, and the present analysis shows that balance to be architecture-specific, not simply a matter of parameter count.

Adding raw audio to video significantly improves Aggregate Accuracy for every model. Transcription produces a significant pool-level gain for Gemma, Gemma-12B, and Qwen-30B, but not for Qwen. Its item-level improvement from 72.50\% to 75.50\% (Section~\ref{subsec:video_transcription}, $p=.023$) does not carry over to its Aggregate Accuracy gain from 38.33\% to 44.33\% ($p=.071$). For this model, transcription improves local, item-level decisions without correcting them systematically enough to raise the number of fully consistent pools to a statistically robust degree: the effect is detectable in isolated predictions but too weak to survive the stricter, semantic-consistency criterion MAIA is specifically designed to enforce. Gemma-12B and Qwen-30B, by contrast, both gain 22 percentage points in Aggregate Accuracy from transcription, remaining highly significant after correction. For these two models, the additional linguistic evidence improves not just how many decisions are correct, but how stable those decisions are across minimally different formulations of the same content.

All four coefficients are negative and statistically significant: Qwen-3B shows the strongest interaction at $\beta_{A \times V}=-0.636$, Qwen-30B the weakest at $-0.298$. A negative coefficient does not mean combining audio and video reduces absolute performance; audio-visual accuracy is higher than video-only accuracy for every model, as already established by the significant McNemar comparisons in Section~\ref{subsec:audio_video}. The coefficient instead indicates that the joint gain is smaller than an additive model of independent modality contributions would predict, pointing to partial informational redundancy or competition between the two channels. Some of what audio contributes may already be recoverable from the video, and the model may fail to preserve the full benefit of each source once both compete within a shared representational or attentional space.
Read together, these two results, a significant audio-visual improvement over video alone and a significant sub-additive interaction, describe two distinct properties of multimodal behaviour rather than contradicting one another. The pairwise tests establish that audio provides \textbf{useful additional evidence whenever video is available}. The interaction coefficient establishes that \textbf{this additional evidence is not combined with the visual channel as an independent, additive source}. Audio and video are complementary, in that both improve performance, but part of their informational content overlaps or is not fully preserved during integration: exactly the distinction between \textit{multimodal benefit} and \textit{multimodal integration} that this thesis has argued for throughout.
The result is compatible with the modality-balancing problem already identified in \textit{Lost in Transcription}, where Qwen2.5-Omni dynamically reduced attention to audio as visual information increased, without the attention still allocated to audio necessarily translating into corresponding gains in reasoning performance \cite{deodati2026lost}. The behavioural results reported here extend this observation across different architectures: additional modalities are used, but their informational contribution stays partly redundant, and their combination falls short of a fully additive effect.
\subsection*{Model Scale and Linguistic Integration}
\label{subsec:model_scale_linguistic}
The influence of model scale varies depending on the input modality. Its impact is least noticeable in video-only scenarios, where the difference between Qwen-3B and Qwen-30B is minimal at 0.75 points. In contrast, the effect is more pronounced in transcription-based contexts, with Qwen-30B surpassing Qwen-3B by 10.50 points when using transcription alone and by 8.58 points when both video and transcription are combined. Similarly, the Gemma-4B family shows that Gemma-12B has a 3.25-point advantage over Gemma-4B in video-only inputs, which increases to 9.67 points with both video and transcription.
Scaling enhances a model's ability to use linguistically structured context effectively. A larger language backbone enables finer lexical distinctions, maintains discourse dependencies better, resolves referential relations more efficiently, and evaluates semantic compatibility between questions, transcriptions, and potential answers—all critical after acoustic information is translated into text. However, visual input presents a different challenge; the model must first derive an informative semantic representation from perceptual evidence before applying linguistic reasoning. Increasing language model capacity doesn't ensure that relevant entities, events, or spatial and temporal relations are correctly extracted.
This asymmetry is supported by preliminary analysis in Section 4, which found that scaling improved scores for entity, event, and inference recovery but did not change the underlying entity-event-inference hierarchy. It is further supported by the video-only bottleneck described in Section 5, which traced the limitation back to what the visual representation contains, independent of post-linguistic reasoning. The original MAIA results support this finding: Qwen2.5-VL-72B significantly outperformed Qwen2.5-VL-7B in a 32-frame pool-based evaluation (0.54 versus 0.44), yet it still faced challenges with temporally demanding categories despite its scaling advantage. This suggests that increased capacity does not consistently resolve perceptual grounding issues.
Overall, these findings highlight that scaling is particularly effective when evidence is already in linguistic form, while improvements with isolated visual inputs remain limited. This asymmetry aligns with a multimodal performance framework based on two distinct capacities: \textbf{extracting task-relevant evidence from a given modality and reasoning over that evidence once it is in a semantically accessible form}. The preliminary analysis in Section \ref{sec:preliminary_analysis_results} primarily addresses the first capacity, while the experimental results discussed here mainly relate to the second. The interaction between both capacities determines how well a model integrates audio, video, and language in Visual Statement Verification.

\subsection*{Attention Allocation and Modality Integration}
\label{subsec:attention_allocation}
The behavioral analyses above establish whether adding an auxiliary modality changes VSV performance. Attention analysis addresses a different question: \textit{how the model distributes its attention during generation when the same visual evidence is combined either with raw audio or with an audio-derived transcription}. The two levels should not be conflated. Attention weights provide a diagnostic view of information access and allocation, but they are not causal attributions and \textbf{do not measure the effective contribution of a modality to the final decision}, their interpretation is therefore most informative when considered together with Conditional Modality Gain and the corresponding Repair and Damage rates.
Figure \ref{fig:attention_allocation} reports the mean attention fractions over the complete autoregressive attention distribution. The values distinguish attention assigned to video, raw audio, transcription, question tokens, the remaining textual context, and tokens generated earlier in the answer. Raw attention fractions are used rather than source-normalized shares in order to preserve a common denominator across models. In particular, the source-normalized estimates obtained for Qwen-30B are not directly comparable with those of the other systems, whereas the raw attention components form a coherent decomposition of the complete attention distribution. A zero value in the question column should likewise be interpreted as a property of the token-span segmentation used for that model, not as evidence that the model ignores the question.

The first relevant result is that \textbf{replacing raw audio with its transcription does not induce a uniform redistribution of attention}: the same transformation produces qualitatively different patterns across models. For Gemma, transcription receives 9.79\% of the total attention budget, compared with 5.99\% for raw audio. This increase is accompanied by a modest reduction in visual attention, from 61.22\% to 59.02\%, and by a reduction in attention to previously generated tokens. The transcription therefore becomes more salient than the native acoustic representation within the same visual context, although this does not establish that transcription contributes more useful evidence to the decision; it shows instead that converting acoustic information into linguistic form changes how strongly that auxiliary source competes with the visual stream for attention.
Gemma-12B exhibits the opposite relation. Raw audio receives 7.34\% of total attention, while transcription receives 4.60\%. Visual allocation remains nearly stable across the two conditions, increasing only from 42.97\% to 43.80\%, and generated-history attention is effectively unchanged at approximately 13.7\%. The substitution of raw audio with text therefore alters the salience of the auxiliary modality without producing a broader reorganization of the model's autoregressive attention regime. Taken together with the Gemma-4B    result, this already excludes a general interpretation in which transcription is systematically more accessible than native audio, or vice versa.
The smaller Qwen-3B model provides an especially useful control for separating attentional allocation from behavioral contribution. Raw audio receives 5.19\% of total attention in the A+V condition, while transcription receives 5.24\% in V+T. Video attention also remains nearly unchanged, from 6.48\% to 6.28\%, as do the remaining textual context and generated history. The model therefore allocates almost the same amount of attention to the auxiliary source independently of whether that source is presented acoustically or textually, so any substantial difference between the corresponding Conditional Modality Gain, Repair Rate, or Damage Rate cannot be explained by a simple difference in the amount of attention devoted to the added modality. It would instead indicate a difference in how the attended representation is transformed into evidence useful for the VSV decision.
Qwen-30B shows the most pronounced condition-dependent reorganization. In the A+V condition, 76.34\% of total attention is assigned to video and 21.69\% to raw audio. The two perceptual sources therefore account for approximately 98.0\% of the measured attention distribution, while generated history receives only 1.60\%. The model is thus clearly capable of allocating substantial attention to native acoustic input when audio and video are jointly available, although high audio attention should not be equated with effective audiovisual reasoning: its behavioral value must still be determined from the paired change in prediction relative to Video Only.
The V+T condition produces a markedly different profile for the same model. Visual attention decreases to 61.53\%, transcription receives only 0.62\%, and attention to previously generated tokens rises from 1.60\% to 36.51\%. The substitution of native audio with transcription therefore coincides with a transition from an attention pattern dominated by external perceptual sources to one with substantially greater allocation to the model's own generated history. This pattern is compatible with stronger autoregressive self-conditioning in the transcription condition, although attention weights alone cannot establish the causal role of this shift. The important result is that raw audio and transcription do not behave as interchangeable carriers of the same semantic content: changing the representational form of the auxiliary signal can reorganize the complete attentional context in which the answer is generated.
Across the four models, the relative ordering between raw audio and transcription is therefore heterogeneous. Gemma-4B   allocates more attention to transcription than to audio, Gemma-12B allocates more to audio than to transcription, Qwen-3B assigns nearly identical attention to the two representations, and Qwen-30B strongly favors native audio. The common thread across models is not a universal preference for one modality, but the dependence of multimodal allocation on the representation through which auxiliary information is supplied. This provides an internal counterpart to the behavioral comparison between Audio+Video and Video+Transcription: the conditional experiments establish whether the additional source changes decisions, while the attention analysis shows that similar behavioral outcomes can arise from different internal allocation patterns and that similar attention levels can support different behavioral effects.
This distinction matters when interpreting modality gain. A large attention share cannot by itself be taken as evidence that the corresponding modality provides discriminative information. Conversely, a small share does not imply that the modality is irrelevant, since a limited amount of highly diagnostic evidence may still alter the decision. Effective multimodal integration is therefore better characterized by the relation between three levels of evidence: attentional allocation, behavioral change, and error transitions. Conditional Modality Gain captures the net change in accuracy, while Repair and Damage rates distinguish useful correction from cross-modal interference. Attention analysis complements these measures by showing whether the additional modality is accessed during generation, but it does not replace them.
The same dissociation was already visible in \textit{Lost in Translation}. In that study, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B allocated a non-negligible part of its attention budget to raw audio even when visual evidence was available, yet the 32-frame configuration changed from 82.70\% without audio to 82.68\% with audio. Raw audio became substantially beneficial only when visual evidence was removed, increasing black-frame accuracy from 67.40\% to 73.19\% \cite{deodati2026lost}. The present analysis extends this observation: what matters is not simply whether a model attends to audio, but whether that attended acoustic information is transformed into discriminative evidence that changes the VSV decision in a useful direction. Absolute attention percentages should not be compared directly across the two studies because the extraction and aggregation procedures differ; the relevant continuity is the qualitative separation between attentional access and behavioral utility.
This interpretation is also consistent with the original design of MAIA. The benchmark was constructed from visual evidence, and audio-based questions were explicitly excluded during annotation \cite{testa2025maia}. Strong allocation to raw audio must therefore be interpreted as exploitation of an auxiliary source that can contain redundant or correlated information about visually defined events, not as evidence that the benchmark itself requires audio. The attention results consequently reinforce the need for the controlled modality ablations used here: a model may devote substantial computational attention to an auxiliary channel even when that channel adds little task-relevant information, while another model may use a smaller attentional share more selectively.
Finally, the attention analysis complements the preliminary visual analysis by operating at a different stage of processing. The preliminary analysis measures whether entities, events, and relations required by a question are recoverable from visual evidence before the question is exposed. The present analysis instead observes allocation during question-conditioned generation, when the task formulation can direct processing toward specific portions of the multimodal context. A high visual attention share therefore does not imply that the visual representation already contains the semantic structure required by the question. Conversely, an incomplete question-independent representation does not prevent the model from later retrieving useful evidence once the linguistic task constrains what should be inspected.
