\section{Preliminary Analysis Results}
\label{sec:preliminary_analysis_results}
\subsection*{Expected Behaviour under the Preliminary Condition}
\label{subsec:preliminary_expectations}
Before examining the experimental outcomes, it is useful to lay out qualitative expectations for model performance under the frame-only condition. This provides a methodological anchor for interpreting distinctions across question categories, rather than deriving explanations retrospectively from the results themselves.
For spatial questions, a relatively high presence of visual evidence is expected, given the inherent visibility of entity locations, movements, object configurations, and spatial relationships within the visual stream. Complexity may increase, however, when this information must be reconstructed across multiple temporal intervals, as when tracking an entity through a sequence of movements or deriving a spatial configuration that results from several successive actions.
Temporal questions are expected to display a more varied profile. Visually distinctive actions and event sequences can often be pinpointed through timestamp data and organized by the temporal structure produced during Event Analysis, but not all temporal information is necessarily recoverable from visual input alone. Some questions may involve events whose interpretation depends on linguistic context, or on relations between moments that remain ambiguous from visual information alone. The adequacy of the resulting representation therefore hinges on the quality of chronological reconstruction as much as on the inherent nature of the events involved.
Causal questions are anticipated to be the most demanding under the visual-only condition. The visual stream can capture event succession, interactions, and observable state changes, but the underlying reasons for these events often depend on spoken dialogue or remain simply indistinguishable from static imagery. Motivations, intentions, and explanatory relationships are less likely to be directly supported by visual evidence alone. This expectation also underlines a methodological distinction maintained throughout the analysis: temporal succession is not treated as evidence of causation.
These expectations are formed independently of the empirical results and serve only as a qualitative reference. No claim is made about the magnitude of category-level differences or about which category will ultimately score highest.
Proficiency results are then compared with downstream Visual Statement Verification (VSV) performance, in order to separate evidence availability from task execution. Visual proficiency asks whether the independently constructed representation contains enough information to distinguish a caption from its foil. VSV performance asks whether the model produces the correct response once the question and the condition-specific inputs are directly available. Together, the two measures offer a diagnostic view that plain task accuracy cannot provide on its own.
A Pass proficiency result paired with a correct VSV response indicates that the relevant visual evidence was both represented and successfully used downstream. A Pass result paired with an incorrect response instead points to a failure occurring after the evidence was already available, in selection, integration, or later reasoning. When both proficiency and VSV performance are negative, the most plausible explanation is a perceptual or representational shortfall, though this comparison alone cannot pinpoint the exact locus of failure.
The most diagnostically informative configuration is the opposite one: proficiency Not Pass paired with a correct downstream answer. Here, the model selects the correct alternative despite a visual representation that does not meet the proficiency criterion, which may reflect partial cues, alternative evidence, parametric priors, shortcut strategies, or simple guessing. The comparison alone cannot isolate which of these mechanisms is at play, but it does establish that correctness and evidence availability are not the same thing, a distinction the sections below return to repeatedly.
\subsection*{Entity, Event, and Inference Recovery}
\label{subsec:entity_event_inference}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pa}, the preliminary analysis asks whether the visual content of a video alone allows a model to recover the information required to answer a question correctly. It also serves an independent diagnostic role by verifying that the newly constructed questions do not inherit the same limitation as the original MAIA benchmark \cite{testa2025maia}, namely a dependence on visual information strong enough to reduce the possibility of assessing genuine cross-modal integration.

The analysis targets an earlier stage of the reasoning process than VSV itself, namely the semantic construction of what has been observed. This makes it possible to determine whether a model can independently extract from the video the entities, events, and inferential relations required by the question. The resulting representation separates information grounded in visual evidence from answers that may instead be supported by linguistic priors.

Entity-level information remains the most accessible representational component for all four models. At this level, the model primarily needs to associate a perceptually available element with its semantic representation, establishing referential grounding between an observed entity and the lexical concept used to describe it. The two larger models obtain the strongest results, with entity-recovery scores of 34.47 for Qwen-30B and 33.77 for Gemma-4-12B. The smaller Gemma model follows the same pattern at a lower absolute level, reaching 27.87.

Qwen-3B represents a clear outlier. Its entity score reaches only 2.77, followed by 2.07 for events and 0.95 for inference, resulting in an overall comprehension score of 2.18. The model therefore remains close to the lower bound of the evaluation despite preserving the same entity--event--inference ordering observed in the other architectures. This result indicates that only a very limited portion of the question-relevant visual information is recovered by its preliminary representation.

The decline from entity recovery to inference recovery is visible in every model and reflects increasing representational demands. Event recovery requires individually detected entities to be integrated into dynamic structures involving actions, participants, and state transitions. This introduces a predicate--argument structure that extends beyond simple referential grounding. Inference recovery requires an additional transformation, since the model must derive relations or interpretations that do not necessarily correspond to a directly observable perceptual pattern. These may involve temporal ordering, causal interpretation, state dependencies, or relations connecting multiple observed events.

Qwen-30B moves from 34.47 for entities to 31.53 for events and 23.95 for inferences. Gemma-4-12B follows a similar pattern, decreasing from 33.77 to 30.07 and then to 21.48. Gemma decreases from 27.87 to 23.13 and finally to 16.48. The same hierarchy is present in Qwen-3B, although at substantially lower values, moving from 2.77 to 2.07 and finally to 0.95.

The comparison therefore suggests two complementary effects. First, increasing representational complexity systematically reduces the amount of recoverable information. Correct entity identification does not imply that the corresponding event has also been represented, and event recovery does not guarantee successful reconstruction of the relation required by the question. Second, the larger variants of both model families recover substantially more information than their smaller counterparts. This difference is especially pronounced within the Qwen family. However, the extremely low Qwen-3B values should be interpreted cautiously, since model size is not the only factor that can affect the quality and completeness of the intermediate structured representation.

Qwen-3B reproduces the same general hierarchy at markedly lower absolute values, indicating that the structure of the task remains visible even when the amount of successfully recovered information is minimal.
\subsection*{Spatial Information}
\label{subsec:preliminary_spatial}
The most successful category is the spatial questions, which achieves 41.50 on entity level, 40.50 on event level, and 37.05 on inference level, for a total comprehension score of 38.85. The relatively minor drop in scores from one level to another is interesting in and of itself. It indicates that once the required entities have been retrieved, the model can retain enough relational information to recreate the spatial configuration required by the question, meaning that spatial relations may often be grounded in observable information in the image.
The same trend is seen with Gemma-4-12B, only at smaller numbers: 35.90 for entities, 33.20 for events, and 28.25 for inferences. Gemma achieves 29.10, 26.10, and 22.60 correspondingly. This category seems to benefit from the perceptual nature of its target relations: locations, relative positions, trajectories, and object configurations can often be detected over a limited period of time, which allows to keep the semantic relation asked for close to perceptual structure.
As far as language is concerned, spatial questions require relating lexicalized spatial concepts such as location, direction, containment, proximity, movement, to geometrical or relational structures in the scene, which requires correctly retrieving the referents and the relation between them. Thus, the better performance of Qwen-30B in the spatial category means that the visual representations provided by this model retain more relational structures. Still, there are cases when the spatial grounding is not successful – the overall comprehension score is under 40 for Qwen-30B, and the visible object does not guarantee retrieving the required relation, especially if it requires tracking the entity through time.
\subsection*{Temporal Information}
\label{subsec:preliminary_temporal}
Of all categories, temporal questions exhibit the most degraded performance in every model that has been tested. For Gemma, this number is 10.60, for Gemma-4-12B 15.05, and for Qwen-30B 13.45. However, this decline does not affect inference alone. Even the representation of entities and events by Gemma is already quite low with the numbers of 15.50 and 12.50, while Gemma-4-12B obtains 20.10 and 19.40, and Qwen-30B obtains 18.30 and 17.60. As far as inference goes, this number is even worse: 7.35, 10.50, and 9.30, respectively.
In other words, temporal difficulty does not become evident at the last reasoning step only. The initial representations tend not to restore one or more entities or events required for the reconstruction of the temporal relation itself. In general, a temporal question relies upon at least two event anchors, which need to be identified in their particularities and arranged accordingly. The absence of one of them means that the temporal relation cannot be restored even though the other one has been identified properly. It can be easily seen in the qualitative analysis, where the model usually recognizes the entities but fails to restore one of the events or anchor it precisely enough for supporting the relations like \textit{before}, \textit{after}, \textit{first}, or \textit{second} to be possible.
As far as the linguistic analysis is concerned, it implies something more than just grounding of lexemes. Events should be represented in their structural semantic form and be placed in the same temporal context, which is exactly what the low scores in inference show. It coincides with the original MAIA study, where temporal phenomena were especially hard to grasp during the visual-only evaluation \cite{testa2025maia}. It also aligns well with the idea behind the benchmarks like VILMA, where temporal grounding is viewed as an important capability separate from static visual recognition.
\subsection*{Causal Information}
\label{subsec:preliminary_causal}
The causal category yields a more varied set of results than expected at first glance. Questions involving causality do not get the lowest scores; Gemma and Gemma-4-12B achieve their highest scores in this category, at 25.79 and 31.75, respectively, and Qwen-30B gets a score of 30.95, which is only slightly lower than the one achieved in the spatial category.
However, this is not indicative of the visual ease of causality. The score distribution gives a very different picture, with both models yielding a much higher score for their entities and events than their inferential component. Thus, Gemma-4-12B achieves an entity score of 45.30 and an event score of 37.60, while its inference score goes down to 25.70. The same applies to Qwen-30B, which yields scores of 43.60, 36.50, and 25.50 in these three categories. Finally, Gemma has the most pronounced drop in proportion, going from an entity score of 39.00 to event 30.80 and inference 19.50. In other words, the visual stream usually contains sufficient information for identifying the participants and the actions that take place in a causal situation; however, it becomes much harder to represent \textit{why} something happens rather than just what happens.
This discrepancy is significant for language understanding because causal reasoning is based on the relationship between propositions or between events, and not a property of a single event. A model may correctly lexicalize an action and identify its participants without representing the explanatory relation between this action and some antecedent state or intention or motivation which was conveyed in spoken form and not visually represented. Therefore, the visual representation may contain the observable result while lacking anything that could help in the causal interpretation. Thus, the current experiment distinguishes between \textit{causal event availability} and \textit{causal relation recovery}.

\subsection*{Model Scale and Visual Representation}
\label{subsec:preliminary_model_scale}
Scale produces a general improvement in preliminary performance, though its magnitude varies by dimension. Gemma-4-12B consistently outperforms the smaller Gemma model, with overall comprehension rising from 20.46 to 25.90, entity recovery from 27.87 to 33.77, event recovery from 23.13 to 30.07, and inference recovery from 16.48 to 21.48.

A larger parameter count therefore does not translate into a uniform gain across every form of visual-semantic reasoning. Model family and representation architecture appear to shape which relational structures are preserved most effectively, a pattern the next section returns to when comparing these preliminary results against the main experiment, where model scale again produces markedly uneven effects depending on which kind of evidence is at stake.
\section{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:experimental_results_discussion}
The experimental conditions provide a controlled view of how linguistic, visual, auditory, and transcription-derived information contribute to Visual Statement Verification. Rather than reading multimodal performance only in terms of absolute accuracy, the analysis below treats each condition as evidence about the informational resources available to the model and about its ability to exploit them consistently, and draws throughout on the preliminary analysis in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_analysis_results} to separate what a model can perceive from what it can do once perception is paired with a question.
The comparison is particularly relevant against the original MAIA results \cite{testa2025maia} and against the experimental setting introduced in \textit{Lost in Transcription} \cite{deodati2026lost}. MAIA was designed as a visually grounded benchmark, in which the distinction between a true statement and its minimally contrastive foil is expected to depend primarily on information extracted from the video. The original evaluation showed that increasing visual evidence generally improves performance, though gains vary considerably across reasoning categories and temporal phenomena remain particularly difficult. \textit{Lost in Transcription} extended this setting to audio and transcription, finding that raw audio contributed substantially when visual evidence was absent but became almost neutral once dense visual information was available, and that adding textualized audio in the transcription-based setting even produced a small but systematic degradation.
The present results paint a different, more heterogeneous picture across model families and scales.

\subsection*{No-Input Condition and Response Bias}

\label{subsec:no_input}

The no-input condition evaluates model behaviour when the question and the two
candidate statements are provided without video, raw audio, or transcription.
This setting requires careful interpretation. Performance above chance shows
that the decision can be made, at least in part, without perceptual evidence,
but it does not identify the mechanism behind that performance. Above-chance
accuracy is not, by itself, evidence of linguistic plausibility.

Prior work on multiple-choice evaluation supports this caution. Large
language models exhibit systematic selection biases toward particular answer
identifiers or positions, and their performance can shift substantially when
the same candidate answers are reordered
\cite{zheng2024largelanguagemodelsrobust,
pezeshkpour-hruschka-2024-large}.
For this reason, no-input accuracy needs to be read alongside the model's
response distribution, not on its own.

The present dataset is globally balanced with respect to answer position:
600 items have the correct answer in the first position and 600 in the second.
A constant strategy selecting either position would therefore achieve exactly
50\% item accuracy. Table~\ref{tab:no_input_bias} reports no-input accuracy
together with the proportion of valid predictions assigned to the first

standard deviation of the two recalls ($RStd$). Following
\cite{zheng2024largelanguagemodelsrobust}, $RStd$ is used as a diagnostic of
selection bias, with larger values indicating a stronger asymmetry between
answer positions.

selecting the same statement regardless of its position. Until that control
is in place, these no-input results are best treated as a diagnostic baseline
for non-perceptual decision behaviour, not as proof that the models are
reasoning from language alone.

\label{subsec:audio_only}

This result matters because MAIA was originally developed as a visually grounded benchmark: its audio track played no role in constructing the questions and statements. Strong audio-only performance does not make the benchmark intrinsically audio-based. It shows instead that the acoustic stream carries information strongly correlated with the events and situations the questions query. Raw audio can supply several complementary sources of evidence at once. Spoken language can explicitly identify objects, actions, locations, intentions, or causal relations. Environmental sounds can signal ongoing events. Acoustic dynamics can offer temporal cues about occurrence or transition.
The linguistic component of speech is likely central to this advantage. Once speech is encoded, its semantic representation interfaces naturally with the language backbone that compares the two candidate statements, narrowing part of the representational gap between visual patterns and linguistic propositions. The result is best read as evidence of high \textit{informational accessibility} in the acoustic channel. It does not mean audio is inherently richer than vision: a visual scene may contain more information overall while still being harder for the architecture to convert into a representation aligned with the VSV decision.
The pattern differs markedly from \textit{Lost in Transcription}, where Qwen2.5-Omni-7B obtained 82.70\% with 32 visual frames and 82.68\% once raw audio was added, a negligible $-0.02$ point difference. There, raw audio became beneficial mainly once visual evidence was removed entirely, raising black-frame accuracy from 67.40\% to 73.19\% \cite{deodati2026lost}. Audio plays a substantially stronger role across the architectures evaluated here. Model-family effects, differences in the reasoning categories considered, and stronger audio exploitation in more recent omnimodal architectures could all contribute. The present results support the behavioural difference without isolating its architectural cause.

Raw audio outperforms transcription for every model, from 7.00 points for Qwen down to 2.17 for Qwen-30B. The contribution of audio cannot be reduced to a simple speech-to-text transformation. Transcription preserves lexical and propositional content, but it strips out or compresses non-verbal sound, prosody, overlapping speakers, temporal acoustic events, and background activity. The transcription pipeline can itself introduce recognition errors or drop uncertain material. The textual representation is a lossy transformation of the original signal.
The gap between the small and large Qwen configurations adds a further, linguistically informative pattern. Qwen reaches 66.50\% with transcription alone, Qwen-30B reaches 77.00\%, a gap considerably larger than the 0.75-point difference the same two models show in video-only input. Increased model capacity is especially beneficial once information is already represented in linguistic form. A larger backbone exploits lexical semantics, discourse relations, event descriptions, and contextual associations more effectively than a smaller one, so scaling gains look more pronounced in language-mediated reasoning than in isolated visual grounding. This fits a more general distinction between perceptual encoding and linguistic inference. Once evidence has been converted to text, what remains is largely semantic selection and integration. Visual evidence, by contrast, must first be turned into a linguistic-semantic representation before the same kind of reasoning can even begin.
This also contrasts with \textit{Lost in Transcription}, where Qwen2.5-VL-7B reached 68.72\% in the linguistic-only baseline but only 63.98\% with transcription alone: transcription failed to outperform the model's own linguistic prior \cite{deodati2026lost}. The models evaluated here instead gain substantially from transcription relative to their no-input baselines, particularly Qwen-30B. The ability to convert auxiliary textual evidence into useful task information appears to vary with scale rather than being a fixed property of an architecture.
\subsection*{Video-Only Performance and the Visual Bottleneck}
\label{subsec:video_only}
Video-only accuracy ranges from 69.83\% to 73.25\%, as already shown in Table~\ref{tab:overall_condition_performance}. All four models extract substantial task-relevant evidence from the sampled frames, yet the narrow range across architectures suggests a shared bottleneck in visual processing rather than a scale-dependent one. Increasing model size from Qwen to Qwen-30B produces only a 0.75-point gain in video-only accuracy, against a 10.50-point gap between the same two models in transcription-only accuracy. Linguistic scale mainly improves operations that run once relevant semantic information is already accessible: lexical comparison, semantic compatibility assessment, integration of explicit contextual evidence, and reasoning over propositions. It does not produce an equivalent improvement in low-level or temporally distributed visual extraction.
This distinction becomes considerably sharper once video-only accuracy is set against the preliminary analysis of Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_analysis_results}. Overall visual comprehension there reached only 20.46 for Gemma, 25.90 for Gemma-4-12B, and 27.75 for Qwen-30B, well below the 69.83\%, 73.08\%, and 73.25\% these same models achieve in video-only VSV.
The discrepancy reflects different informational regimes rather than a contradiction between the two evaluations. The preliminary analysis constructs a representation with no access to the downstream question. It asks whether the required entities, events, and relations are already present in a question-independent description of the video. The VSV condition instead gives the model the question together with two minimally contrastive alternatives. Visual processing can then become explicitly conditioned on the linguistic target of the task, directing attention toward particular entities, actions, or relations. The contrastive alternatives also narrow the decision space enough that a full, exhaustive semantic representation of the video is not strictly necessary to choose correctly.
A low preliminary score shows that the relevant evidence is not reliably present in a model's spontaneous visual representation. It does not show that the same evidence can never be recovered once the question identifies what to look for. The distinction can be summarised as one between \textit{question-independent representation} and \textit{question-conditioned retrieval}: the former measures what is represented before any task-specific linguistic guidance, the latter what the question itself can pull out of the visual input once it acts as a retrieval constraint.
The original MAIA study offers a useful external reference point. With 32 frames, Qwen2.5-VL-7B obtained a pool-based VSV accuracy of 0.44, and Qwen2.5-VL-72B reached 0.54 \cite{testa2025maia}. This is a clear scaling advantage, but it fell well short of full consistency: categories such as Spatial Total, Causal, and Implicit Total improved strongly with richer visual evidence, while temporal reasoning remained comparatively weak. Direct numerical comparison with the present models must stay cautious, since the current evaluation covers a different category subset and different architectures. The qualitative pattern, though, is the same. Visual evidence improves performance substantially, yet stronger language-model capacity alone does not eliminate the difficulty of grounding temporally and relationally complex information. The preliminary analysis locates that difficulty specifically at the level of what gets extracted from the frames in the first place.
\subsection*{Joint Analysis of Preliminary Representation and Video-Only Performance}
\label{subsec:preliminary_video_only}

This second analysis preserves the semantic correspondence between the evidence evaluated during the preliminary stage and the downstream decision.
The preliminary representation was characterized through entity recovery, event recovery, inference recovery, and overall comprehension. Downstream performance was measured using both item-level accuracy and Aggregate Accuracy. Since preliminary scores are bounded continuous variables while video-level VSV scores are discrete, Spearman's rank correlation was adopted as the primary measure of association.

\subsubsection*{Question-Independent Representation and Question-Conditioned Retrieval}
The joint analysis identifies a clear distinction between the availability of visual evidence before question exposure and successful downstream task execution.
For Gemma, Gemma-4-12B, and Qwen-30B, reaching the preliminary Pass threshold is associated with a substantial increase in downstream item accuracy. Qwen-30B shows the largest jump, from 70.3\% under Not Pass to 87.5\% under Pass.
This confirms that the quality of the question-independent representation contributes to downstream success.
The relationship is nevertheless not deterministic. The relatively weak video-level correlations and the high accuracy observed under Not Pass conditions show that successful VSV execution does not require all relevant information to have already been recovered during the preliminary stage.
The distinction follows from the informational structure of the two experimental settings. In the preliminary condition, visual representation is constructed before the model has access to the question. The model must encode the video without knowing which entities, events, or relations will subsequently become relevant.
In the video-only VSV condition, the question and the minimally contrastive alternatives explicitly identify the semantic distinction that must be resolved. The linguistic input can therefore guide the selection of visual evidence toward task-relevant portions of the video.
The preliminary analysis can consequently be interpreted as a measure of \textit{question-independent evidence availability}, whereas downstream video-only performance reflects a broader process that includes visual perception, question-conditioned evidence selection, semantic comparison, and linguistic reasoning.
Qwen represents the most extreme manifestation of this distinction. None of its preliminary representations reaches the Pass threshold, yet the model achieves 72.5\% item accuracy and 38.3\% Aggregate Accuracy in the video-only condition. Its downstream performance is clearly not a direct reflection of what was recovered during the question-independent preliminary stage.
The Qwen result must also be interpreted methodologically. Since almost all of its preliminary observations are concentrated at the lowest proficiency level, correlation-based analyses have limited discriminative power. The absence of significant associations for this model reflects both weak preliminary recovery and restricted score variability.
Across the four models, the statistical evidence therefore supports a distinction between \textit{having already represented the necessary visual evidence} and \textit{being able to recover or exploit sufficient evidence once the task is specified}. Preliminary proficiency raises the probability of downstream success. It is not a necessary condition for correct VSV performance.

\subsection*{Raw Audio and Transcription as Complementary Representations}
\label{subsec:audio_vs_textualized_audio}
The purely descriptive ranking between the two multimodal conditions suggests a split by model size. For Gemma and Qwen, raw audio gives the strongest multimodal configuration, 77.17\% against 75.08\% for Gemma and 78.92\% against 75.50\% for Qwen. For the larger configurations the ranking flips, 84.75\% against 80.50\% for Gemma-4-12B and 84.08\% against 83.17\% for Qwen-30B.

Once tested directly, this small-versus-large story does not hold up cleanly. Gemma's numerical advantage for raw audio and Qwen-30B's numerical advantage for transcription both fail to reach significance ($p=.153$ and $p=.351$), so for these two models the item-level data cannot statistically distinguish between the two multimodal representations. A significant, representation-dependent preference emerges only for the remaining two models, and it does not line up neatly with scale: Gemma-4-12B performs significantly better with transcription-derived linguistic information ($\Delta=+4.25$ points, $p=.001$), while Qwen shows the opposite pattern, with native raw audio outperforming transcription by 3.42 points ($p=.009$).
The data therefore do not support a general claim that smaller models systematically prefer raw audio and larger models systematically prefer transcription. The advantage of one representation over the other is model-dependent. It likely reflects the specific interaction between a model's modality-specific encoders and the linguistic reasoning capacity of its backbone, more than any property of scale on its own.
This also clarifies why transcription should not be treated as a simple stand-in for audio. Raw audio preserves acoustic and temporal information at the cost of requiring cross-modal representation alignment. Transcription discards part of that information in exchange for direct access to lexical and propositional structure. Which of the two wins out for a given model depends on the balance it strikes between perceptual richness and linguistic accessibility, and this analysis shows that balance to be architecture-specific, not simply a matter of parameter count.

\subsection*{Audio--Video Interaction Effect}
\label{subsec:interaction_effect}
Positive multimodal gains do not by themselves imply that audio and video combine independently. A logistic interaction analysis over the $2\times2$ factorial design of no-input, audio-only, video-only, and audio-visual conditions returns a negative interaction coefficient for every model.

    \label{tab:audio_video_interaction}

All four coefficients are negative and statistically significant: Qwen shows the strongest interaction at $\beta_{A \times V}=-0.636$, Qwen-30B the weakest at $-0.298$. A negative coefficient does not mean combining audio and video reduces absolute performance. Audio-visual accuracy is higher than video-only accuracy for every model, as already established by the significant McNemar comparisons in Section~\ref{subsec:audio_video}. What the coefficient means instead is that the joint gain is smaller than an additive model of independent modality contributions would predict. This points to partial informational redundancy or competition between the two channels. Some of what audio contributes may already be recoverable from the video, and the model may fail to preserve the full benefit of each source once both compete within a shared representational or attentional space.
Read together, these two results, a significant audio-visual improvement over video alone and a significant sub-additive interaction, describe two distinct properties of multimodal behaviour. They do not contradict one another. The pairwise tests establish that audio provides useful additional evidence whenever video is available. The interaction coefficient establishes that this additional evidence is not combined with the visual channel as an independent, additive source. Audio and video are complementary, in that both improve performance, but part of their informational content overlaps or is not fully preserved during integration. This is exactly the distinction between \textit{multimodal benefit} and \textit{multimodal integration} that this thesis has argued for throughout.
This result is compatible with the modality-balancing problem already identified in \textit{Lost in Transcription}, where Qwen2.5-Omni dynamically reduced attention to audio as visual information increased, without the attention still allocated to audio necessarily translating into corresponding gains in reasoning performance \cite{deodati2026lost}. The behavioural results reported here extend this observation across different architectures: additional modalities are used, but their informational contribution stays partly redundant, and their combination falls short of a fully additive effect.
\subsection*{Model Scale and Linguistic Integration}
\label{subsec:model_scale_linguistic}
Model scale affects the different input modalities unevenly. Its weakest effect appears in the video-only condition, where Qwen and Qwen-30B differ by only 0.75 points. Its strongest effect appears in transcription-based conditions, where Qwen-30B exceeds Qwen by 10.50 points with transcription alone and by 8.58 points with video plus transcription. The Gemma family shows the same tendency: a 3.25-point advantage for Gemma-4-12B over Gemma in video-only input widens to 9.67 points in video plus transcription.
Scaling disproportionately improves the exploitation of linguistically structured contextual evidence. A larger language backbone encodes finer lexical distinctions, maintains longer discourse dependencies, resolves referential relations more effectively, and evaluates semantic compatibility between the question, the transcription, and the candidate statements. All of this matters once acoustic information has already been converted into text. Visual input poses a different problem. Before any of that linguistic reasoning can apply, the model has to derive a sufficiently informative semantic representation from perceptual evidence in the first place. Increasing language-model capacity offers no guarantee that the relevant entities, events, spatial relations, or temporal dependencies were correctly extracted to begin with.
This asymmetry is directly supported by the preliminary analysis in Section~\ref{subsec:preliminary_model_scale}, where scale raised entity, event, and inference recovery scores but never removed the underlying entity-event-inference hierarchy. It is also supported by the video-only bottleneck discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:video_only}, which traced this same limitation back to what the visual representation itself contains, independently of any linguistic reasoning applied afterward. The original MAIA results point in the same direction. Qwen2.5-VL-72B substantially outperformed Qwen2.5-VL-7B in the 32-frame pool-based evaluation, 0.54 against 0.44 \cite{testa2025maia}, yet continued to report weak performance on temporally demanding categories despite this scaling advantage. This suggests that increased overall capacity does not uniformly resolve perceptual grounding limitations.
Taken together, these results sharpen this picture considerably. Scaling is especially effective once evidence is already available in linguistic form, while gains on isolated visual input stay comparatively constrained. This asymmetry is consistent with an account of multimodal performance resting on at least two separable capacities: extracting task-relevant evidence from a given modality, and reasoning over that evidence once it has been mapped into a semantically accessible representation. The preliminary analysis in Section~\ref{sec:preliminary_analysis_results} speaks primarily to the first capacity. The experimental results discussed in this section speak primarily to the second. It is the interaction between the two, more than either one alone, that determines how well a given model ultimately integrates audio, video, and language in Visual Statement Verification.
